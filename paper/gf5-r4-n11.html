The rank-four, eleven-element excluded minors
for representability over 𝔽5\mathbb F_5

A computational classification
Revised exposition, 5 November 2026

Abstract

There are exactly 35 isomorphism classes of rank-four, eleven-element excluded minors for representability over 𝔽5\mathbb F_5. The proof is an exhaustive construction. Every such matroid has a representable ten-element deletion. For each possible deletion, we recover all nonrepresentable extensions whose other deletions are representable, then test their contractions. The essential observation is that, when the ground set has more than twice the rank many elements, compatible basis data for the single-element deletions already determine a matroid. This turns the extension search into an exact intersection of finite tables. We prove that each stage preserves every possible excluded minor, explain how the tables are constructed and exhausted, and give the computational results that complete the classification. Separate certificates verify nonrepresentability and all immediate minors of each of the 35 resulting matroids.

1. The theorem and the structure of its proof

A matroid is quinary if it is representable over 𝔽5=ℤ/5ℤ\mathbb F_5=\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z. An excluded minor for quinary matroids is a nonquinary matroid every proper minor of which is quinary. We classify these obstructions at one specified rank and size.

Theorem 1.1 (Computational classification). There are exactly 35 isomorphism classes of rank-four, eleven-element excluded minors for representability over 𝔽5\mathbb F_5. Representatives M1,…,M35M_1,\ldots,M_{35} are specified in Appendix B.

The defining condition has a useful finite form. A matroid MM is an excluded minor precisely when M is nonquinary,M\a and M/a are quinary for every a∈E(M).M\text{ is nonquinary},\qquad M\setminus a\text{ and }M/a\text{ are quinary for every }a\in E(M). Indeed, every proper minor is a minor of one of these immediate deletions or contractions, and field representability is closed under taking minors. For an eleven-element matroid there are therefore 22 immediate minors to consider. This criterion is easy to apply to a given matroid. The main difficulty is to find a finite list that is guaranteed to contain every matroid satisfying it.

We construct such a list by reversing deletion. Choose an element ee of an unknown excluded minor MM and put N=M\eN=M\setminus e. The matroid NN is quinary. We call it a parent, since the task is to recover MM by adding ee to it. The proof proceeds in three steps.

  1. Find all possible parents. We prove that NN must be a connected simple rank-four matroid on ten elements, and generate a complete quinary catalogue of these matroids (Section 2).

  2. Recover all possible extensions of each parent. For a fixed NN, we construct exactly the nonquinary extensions MM for which every deletion M\aM\setminus a is quinary. The recovery theorem is proved in Section 3; its finite tables and their exhaustive solution are developed in Section 4.

  3. Impose the remaining minor conditions. We test every contraction of every recovered matroid and identify isomorphic outputs. This leaves precisely the desired excluded minors (Section 5).

The second step is the heart of the argument. A quinary extension of NN can be obtained by appending a column to a representation of NN; the desired MM, however, is nonquinary. Instead we construct its different deletions from representations, and require their basis data to agree wherever they overlap. A local-to-global lemma then shows that these compatible data form a matroid. We can thus enumerate nonrepresentable matroids using only finite lists of representable ones.

Two inclusions will finish the proof. The construction must contain every excluded minor, giving an upper bound on their number. Each listed object must also be an excluded minor, and the objects must be pairwise nonisomorphic, giving the matching lower bound. The computation in Section 6 supplies both: the exhaustive construction leaves 35 classes, and separate certificates verify all 35. Its numerical results are organized there according to the three steps above; implementation records are collected in Appendix C.

For comparison, Brettell’s classification through ten elements gives 5, 92, 219, and 130 rank-four excluded minors at sizes seven through ten [1]. We reproduce those smaller catalogues as checks on the method. The use of compatible extensions and of inequivalent representations has precedents in the computations of Brettell and Pendavingh [2]. Here the proof of coverage rests on generating all possible parents and imposing compatibility with every deletion.

2. Step 1: finding every possible parent

This step establishes the starting point of the search: a complete list of matroids NN that could occur as M\eM\setminus e. We first determine which parents are needed, then prove that the represented generation procedure reaches all of them.

2.1. Why connected simple parents suffice

We use the standard notation rM(X)r_M(X) for the rank of a subset and ℬ(M)\mathcal B(M) for the bases of MM. All matroids are finite. A simple matroid has no loops or parallel pairs.

Lemma 2.1 (Possible deletions). If MM is a rank-four, eleven-element excluded minor for quinary matroids, then for every e∈E(M)e\in E(M) the deletion M\eM\setminus e is a connected simple rank-four quinary matroid on ten elements.

Proof. An excluded minor for representability over a field is 3-connected when it has at least four elements. To recall the reason, representability over a fixed field is closed under direct sums and 2-sums. A disconnected matroid decomposes as a direct sum; a connected matroid with a 2-separation decomposes as a 2-sum. The summands are isomorphic to proper minors [3]. If the matroid were an excluded minor, all summands would be representable, forcing the matroid itself to be representable. For the 2-sum construction, choose representations in which the common element is a unit vector, identify its one-dimensional span in the two vector spaces, and delete its column.

Thus MM is 3-connected. It has no loops, parallel pairs, or coloops, so N=M\eN=M\setminus e is simple and has rank four. It is quinary by minor-minimality. It remains to prove that NN is connected. If E(N)=A∪̇BE(N)=A\mathbin{\dot\cup}B were a nontrivial direct-sum partition, choose the names so that |A|≥2|A|\geq2. Since r(N)=r(M)r(N)=r(M), we would have rM(A)+rM(B)=r(M),rM(A)+rM(B∪{e})−r(M)≤1.r_M(A)+r_M(B)=r(M),\qquad r_M(A)+r_M(B\cup\{e\})-r(M)\leq1. Both AA and B∪{e}B\cup\{e\} have at least two elements. This contradicts 3-connectivity of MM. ▫\square

Let P={0,…,9}P=\{0,\ldots,9\} and take a new label e=10e=10. Let 𝒫\mathcal P contain one labelled representative on PP of each connected simple rank-four quinary matroid on ten elements. For each N∈𝒫N\in\mathcal P, define 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N) to be the set of simple rank-four matroids MM on P∪{e}P\cup\{e\} satisfying M\e=N,M is nonquinary,M\a is quinary for every a∈P∪{e}.\begin{gathered} M\setminus e=N,\qquad M\text{ is nonquinary},\\ M\setminus a\text{ is quinary for every }a\in P\cup\{e\}. \end{gathered} We call these deletion-minimal nonquinary extensions of NN. The word “deletion” is essential: contractions have not yet been tested.

Proposition 2.2 (Reduction of the classification). The rank-four, eleven-element quinary excluded minors are exactly, up to isomorphism, the members of ⋃N∈𝒫{M∈𝒟(N):M/a is quinary for every a∈E(M)}.\bigcup_{N\in\mathcal P} \{M\in\mathcal D(N):M/a\text{ is quinary for every }a\in E(M)\}.

Proof. Every matroid in the displayed set is nonquinary and has quinary immediate deletions and contractions, so it is an excluded minor. Conversely, let MM be any excluded minor of the specified rank and size. By Lemma 2.1, a deletion M\eM\setminus e is isomorphic to a member NN of 𝒫\mathcal P. Relabel this deletion as NN and its missing element as ee. The resulting matroid belongs to 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N), and all its contractions are quinary. Its isomorphism class therefore occurs in the displayed set. ▫\square

This proposition specifies exactly what the rest of the computation must produce. It suffices to construct 𝒫\mathcal P, compute each 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N) completely, and apply the contraction test. In particular, a parent may be discarded for being disconnected, but not merely for failing to be 3-connected.

2.2. Generating all simple quinary parents

The aim is to list, up to matroid isomorphism, all simple rank-four quinary matroids on ten elements, and then retain the connected ones. We describe the construction for a general rank rr and target size mm; here r=4r=4 and m=10m=10. Throughout this construction, kk denotes the current number of elements, ranging from rr to mm. The eleven-element nonquinary extensions are constructed in Step 2, not by this matrix enumeration.

A rank-rr quinary matroid on kk elements has a full-rank r×kr\times k representation over 𝔽5\mathbb F_5, with one column per element. Simplicity means that no column is zero and no two columns are scalar multiples. Thus its columns specify distinct points of PG(r−1,5)\operatorname{PG}(r-1,5). We represent each projective point by the vector whose first nonzero entry is 1. There are (5r−1)/4(5^r-1)/4 such points, in particular 156 in rank four.

Two matrices are field-isomorphic if one can be obtained from the other by invertible row operations, nonzero column scalings, and a permutation of columns. Field-isomorphic matrices represent isomorphic matroids, but the converse can fail. During growth we must retain one matrix from every field-isomorphism class, even when several such classes represent the same abstract matroid. The proof below explains why field isomorphism permits safe removal of repetitions: it preserves all possibilities for adding a column.

Every full-rank representation contains rr columns forming a basis. Move them to the front and apply row operations to make them IrI_r. This explains both the starting matrix and the growth procedure:

  1. Start at size k=rk=r with the single matrix IrI_r.

  2. From every retained r×kr\times k matrix, append each projective point not already among its columns. These are all its simple represented extensions of rank rr and size k+1k+1.

  3. Among all matrices produced at size k+1k+1, retain one per field-isomorphism class. Repeat until size mm is complete.

There are no levels k<rk<r: a rank-rr matroid needs at least rr elements. The smaller levels r,…,m−1r,\ldots,m-1 are the intermediate stages used to construct the target level, rather than enumerating all sets of mm projective points directly.

The implementation tests field isomorphism using Sage’s is_field_isomorphic. It searches for a column bijection under which the representations agree up to row operations and column scaling. For a proposed bijection, corresponding bases are reduced to identity form, after which consistency of row and column scalings can be checked over 𝔽5\mathbb F_5. Candidates are first grouped by an invariant of their basis families to reduce comparisons; matching invariants alone never causes a matrix to be discarded.

Proposition 2.3 (Completeness of represented generation). For every k=r,…,mk=r,\ldots,m, the procedure retains a matrix from every field-isomorphism class of simple rank-rr quinary representations on kk elements. Consequently, their underlying matroids include every simple rank-rr, kk-element quinary matroid up to isomorphism.

Proof. Fix a simple rank-rr representation AA on s≤ms\leq m elements. Choose a basis, move its columns to the front, and transform them to IrI_r. Order the remaining columns, and let AkA_k be the first kk columns, for r≤k≤sr\leq k\leq s. Thus Ar=IrA_r=I_r and As=AA_s=A.

We prove by induction on kk that the retained level at size kk contains a matrix field-isomorphic to AkA_k. At size rr this is the starting matrix IrI_r. Suppose the claim holds at size k<sk<s, and let BkB_k be a retained matrix field-isomorphic to AkA_k. Write Ak+1=[Ak∣v]A_{k+1}=[A_k\mid v]. Let TT be the invertible row transformation in an equivalence taking AkA_k to BkB_k, the other operations being scalings and a permutation of its old columns. Then [Bk∣Tv]is field-isomorphic to[Ak∣v].[B_k\mid Tv]\quad\text{is field-isomorphic to}\quad[A_k\mid v]. Indeed, apply the same row transformation to the extended matrix, and extend the old column operations by leaving the new column fixed. Normalize TvTv to represent its projective point. This point is unused in BkB_k: otherwise invertibility of TT would imply that vv is parallel to an old column of AkA_k, contrary to simplicity.

The procedure tries every unused projective point, so it generates this extension of BkB_k. Deduplication retains either that matrix or a field-isomorphic one. This proves the induction step and hence the claim at size ss. Since AA and ss were arbitrary, the assertion follows. ▫\square

To obtain the abstract catalogue from a completed represented level, replace each matrix AA by its basis family ℬ(A)={S⊆{0,…,k−1}:|S|=r,det(AS)≠0},\mathcal B(A)=\{S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,k-1\}:|S|=r,\ \det(A_S)\neq0\}, where ASA_S consists of the columns indexed by SS. A basis family specifies the whole matroid without using coordinates. Two such families define isomorphic matroids exactly when a permutation of the elements takes one family to the other. Sage’s exact is_isomorphic test on basis matroids removes these abstract repetitions, again using basis invariants only to group comparisons. Each retained abstract matroid is stored as one bit for each rr-subset in lexicographic order, recording whether it is a basis; for a ten-element rank-four parent this uses (104)=210\binom{10}{4}=210 bits, stored in hexadecimal.

The matrix catalogue is saved separately and remains the input for further represented growth and the labelled representation lists in Section 4. Thus abstract deduplication does not discard any represented class. At size ten, we retain the connected abstract rank-four matroids as 𝒫\mathcal P. The same generation procedure in rank three supplies the catalogue used to test contractions in Section 5.

Step 1 is now justified: every possible deletion of an excluded minor occurs in 𝒫\mathcal P. We next explain how to recover its possible nonquinary extensions. This requires more than appending columns to the parent itself; it requires matching the representable deletions of the prospective extension.

3. Step 2: recovering an extension from its deletions

Fix one parent N∈𝒫N\in\mathcal P. Our goal in this section is an exact description of 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N) that can be computed from finite lists of quinary matroids. We first describe the unknown data of an extension, then express the conditions on its deletions in those data. The main proposition will show that these conditions already enforce the matroid axioms.

3.1. The unknown basis data

The deletion equation M\e=NM\setminus e=N fixes the status of every four-set avoiding ee: it is a basis of MM exactly when it is a basis of NN. The only unknown four-sets are therefore T∪{e},T∈(P3).T\cup\{e\},\qquad T\in\binom P3. There are (103)=120\binom{10}{3}=120 of these, compared with the (104)=210\binom{10}{4}=210 already fixed four-sets. Introduce a Boolean variable xTx_T for each triple, with xT=1x_T=1 meaning that T∪{e}T\cup\{e\} is a basis. An assignment xx specifies a nonempty four-subset family ℬN(x)=ℬ(N)∪{T∪{e}:T∈(P3),xT=1}.\mathcal B_N(x)=\mathcal B(N)\ \cup \{T\cup\{e\}:T\in\binom P3,\ x_T=1\}. For the moment this is only a proposed basis family; an arbitrary assignment need not define a matroid.

Consider how another deletion reads these variables. For f∈Pf\in P, the four-sets of M\fM\setminus f that contain ee are indexed by the triples avoiding ff. Write πf(x)=x|(P\{f}3)\pi_f(x)=x\big|_{\binom{P\setminus\{f\}}3} for the restriction to those coordinates. The four-sets avoiding both ee and ff are already determined by N\fN\setminus f. Thus πf(x)\pi_f(x) is exactly the extra basis data needed to specify M\fM\setminus f as an extension of N\fN\setminus f.

For example, the bit x{0,1,2}x_{\{0,1,2\}} records whether {0,1,2,10}\{0,1,2,10\} is a basis. This bit is visible in the deletion of 3 and in the deletion of 4, but disappears in the deletion of 0. If independently chosen extensions for the deletions of 3 and 4 disagree on that bit, they cannot be deletions of one matroid. A single assignment xx enforces agreement on every such shared four-set at once.

To express which deletions are permitted, let QQ be any fixed labelled simple rank-four quinary matroid on a set UU. Define its represented extension table ℛ(Q)\mathcal R(Q) to be the set of Boolean patterns zz on (U3)\binom U3 for which ℬ(Q)∪{T∪{e}:zT=1}\mathcal B(Q)\ \cup\{T\cup\{e\}:z_T=1\} is the basis family of a simple rank-four quinary extension of QQ. All labels in this definition are fixed. In Section 4 we will construct this table completely from representations of QQ.

The meaning of table membership is now precise:

  • πf(x)∈ℛ(N\f)\pi_f(x)\in\mathcal R(N\setminus f) says that the proposed deletion avoiding ff is simple, rank four, and quinary;

  • x∈ℛ(N)x\in\mathcal R(N) says that the proposed full extension of NN is itself simple, rank four, and quinary.

The first statement is meaningful because a connected parent has no coloops, so every N\fN\setminus f still has rank four.

Consequently the assignments we want should form the finite set 𝒮(N)={x∈{0,1}(P3):πf(x)∈ℛ(N\f) for every f∈P}\ℛ(N).\mathcal S(N)= \bigl\{x\in\{0,1\}^{\binom P3}: \pi_f(x)\in\mathcal R(N\setminus f)\text{ for every }f\in P\bigr\} \setminus\mathcal R(N). The intersection requires all the other deletions to be quinary; the subtraction removes the extensions that are quinary themselves. What still needs proof is that an assignment satisfying these conditions actually defines a matroid. Without that implication, the displayed expression would merely describe consistent tables of four-subsets.

3.2. Why compatible deletions force the matroid axioms

The basis-exchange axiom involves two bases and hence at most twice the rank many elements. If the ground set is larger, the whole exchange question can be seen inside one deletion. This gives the needed local-to-global principle.

Lemma 3.1 (Deletion compatibility). Let |E|=n>2r|E|=n>2r and let ⌀≠ℬ⊆(Er)\varnothing\ne\mathcal B\subseteq\binom Er. Suppose that for each a∈Ea\in E the family of members of ℬ\mathcal B avoiding aa is the basis family of a rank-rr matroid on E\{a}E\setminus\{a\}. Then ℬ\mathcal B is the basis family of a rank-rr matroid on EE.

Proof. Take A,B∈ℬA,B\in\mathcal B and b∈A\Bb\in A\setminus B. We must find c∈B\Ac\in B\setminus A such that (A\{b})∪{c}∈ℬ(A\setminus\{b\})\cup\{c\}\in\mathcal B. Since |A∪B|≤2r<n|A\cup B|\leq2r<n, there is an element a∉A∪Ba\notin A\cup B. Both AA and BB are bases of the specified deletion avoiding aa. Basis exchange in that matroid supplies the required cc, and its exchanged basis also belongs to ℬ\mathcal B. This proves every ordered instance of basis exchange. Together with nonemptiness and the common size of the members, it proves that ℬ\mathcal B is a matroid basis family. ▫\square

Here n=11n=11 and r=4r=4, so the lemma applies. In particular, the representations witnessing different deletions do not have to fit together as matrices. They need only prescribe compatible bases. The lemma then supplies the abstract matroid, even when it has no representation over 𝔽5\mathbb F_5.

Proposition 3.2 (Exact recovery of deletion-minimal extensions). For a fixed parent N∈𝒫N\in\mathcal P, the map x↦Mxx\mapsto M_x, where ℬ(Mx)=ℬN(x)\mathcal B(M_x)=\mathcal B_N(x), is a bijection from 𝒮(N)\mathcal S(N) to 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N).

Proof. First take x∈𝒮(N)x\in\mathcal S(N). The proposed basis family ℬN(x)\mathcal B_N(x) is nonempty because it contains ℬ(N)\mathcal B(N). Its deletion avoiding ee is the rank-four quinary matroid NN. For each f∈Pf\in P, the condition πf(x)∈ℛ(N\f)\pi_f(x)\in\mathcal R(N\setminus f) says that its deletion avoiding ff is also the basis family of a simple rank-four quinary matroid. Lemma 3.1 therefore makes ℬN(x)\mathcal B_N(x) a matroid basis family.

Call this matroid MxM_x. Each pair of elements lies in one of its simple deletions, so MxM_x is simple. All its deletions are quinary by construction. If MxM_x were quinary, its pattern would belong to ℛ(N)\mathcal R(N) by the definition of that table, contrary to the subtraction in 𝒮(N)\mathcal S(N). Hence Mx∈𝒟(N)M_x\in\mathcal D(N).

Conversely, take M∈𝒟(N)M\in\mathcal D(N). Its bases containing ee give a unique pattern xx. For every f∈Pf\in P, the matroid M\fM\setminus f is a simple quinary extension of N\fN\setminus f; it has rank four since it contains the rank-four restriction N\fN\setminus f. Thus πf(x)∈ℛ(N\f)\pi_f(x)\in\mathcal R(N\setminus f). Since MM is nonquinary, x∉ℛ(N)x\notin\mathcal R(N). Therefore x∈𝒮(N)x\in\mathcal S(N), and M=MxM=M_x. Finally, different patterns give different basis families, proving injectivity. ▫\square

This proposition is the bridge between the mathematical problem and the computation. It accounts for all four requirements on an output: validity as a matroid, simplicity, representability of every deletion, and nonrepresentability of the full matroid. To finish Step 2, it remains to build the tables ℛ(Q)\mathcal R(Q) without omissions and exhaust the finite conditions defining 𝒮(N)\mathcal S(N).

4. Constructing and exhausting the extension tables

Proposition 3.2 reduces each parent search to a finite problem about quinary extensions of nine- and ten-element matroids. There are two obligations: every represented extension must occur in its table, and every simultaneous solution of the table conditions must be considered. We address them in that order.

4.1. Putting all representations on the required labels

The represented catalogues of Section 2 retain all field-isomorphism classes. A table ℛ(Q)\mathcal R(Q), however, describes extensions of one labelled matroid QQ. To use the catalogue for this purpose, we need all representations on those labels, up to row operations and column scaling alone. We call the latter equivalence labelled projective equivalence.

First choose one matroid isomorphism aligning each unlabelled represented class of QQ with the chosen labels. There may be many alignments, and they can give different labelled extension patterns. The missing alignments are recovered by applying all automorphisms of QQ to the aligned matrices.

Lemma 4.1 (Complete labelled representations). Align one matrix from every field-isomorphism class representing QQ with the fixed labels of QQ, and close the resulting set under Aut(Q)\operatorname{Aut}(Q). Up to labelled projective equivalence, this gives every representation of the labelled matroid QQ.

Proof. Take any representation on those labels and forget its labels. It belongs to one of the represented classes in the complete catalogue. Choose a field isomorphism from the catalogue matrix to this representation. It induces an alignment of the underlying matroid with QQ. The chosen alignment of that same catalogue matrix differs from it by an automorphism of QQ. Applying that automorphism to the aligned matrix therefore reaches the desired representation, up to row operations and column scaling. ▫\square

The computation closes under generators of the full automorphism group, which reaches the same orbit as applying the entire group. Repeated labelled projective classes are recognized by a canonical matrix normalization. The normalization and its proof are given in Appendix A.1; its purpose is to remove repetitions without discarding a representation class. Denote the resulting complete labelled set of matrices by ℒ(Q)\mathcal L(Q).

4.2. Building a table by appending projective points

For each V∈ℒ(Q)V\in\mathcal L(Q) append every point vv of PG(3,5)\operatorname{PG}(3,5) whose direction is not already a column of VV. The resulting matrix has rank four, and its column matroid is simple. Record the pattern ρ(V,v)T=1⇔det[VT∣v]≠0,T∈(E(Q)3),\rho(V,v)_T=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \det[\,V_T\mid v\,]\ne0, \qquad T\in\binom{E(Q)}3, where VTV_T consists of the three old columns indexed by TT. Collect all these rows, merging duplicates.

Proposition 4.2 (Completeness of the extension table). The rows obtained in this way are exactly ℛ(Q)\mathcal R(Q).

Proof. Every appended point gives a simple rank-four quinary extension, so every constructed row belongs to ℛ(Q)\mathcal R(Q). Conversely, take any extension represented by a row of ℛ(Q)\mathcal R(Q), and choose a matrix representing it. Its restriction to QQ is labelled projectively equivalent to a member VV of ℒ(Q)\mathcal L(Q), by Lemma 4.1. Apply that equivalence to the entire matrix. The new column is a nonzero projective point not already used, since the extension is simple. That point is among those appended to VV, so the extension’s row occurs in the construction. ▫\square

This proves the first obligation of the section. In particular, a deletion N\fN\setminus f is matched to its catalogue representative and all its matrices are aligned back to the actual labels P\{f}P\setminus\{f\} before its table is formed. Equality of bits in different tables consequently refers to the same elements, as required by Proposition 3.2.

All determinant calculations take place in 𝔽5\mathbb F_5. For a fixed old triple the determinant is a linear form in vv, so its cofactors allow all 156 projective points to be tested in one finite calculation. This speeds up table construction while computing exactly the determinant bits in the displayed definition.

4.3. Compressing the coordinates without changing the solutions

The 120 triple coordinates give the simplest proof of the recovery theorem, but many must agree. We now identify those repetitions to obtain the smaller problems actually solved by the program.

Let MM be any rank-preserving matroid extension of NN. If a triple TT is dependent in NN, then rM(T∪{e})≤3r_M(T\cup\{e\})\leq3, so its bit is zero. If TT is independent and H=clN(T)H=\operatorname{cl}_N(T), then HH is a rank-three hyperplane and clM(T)=clM(H).\operatorname{cl}_M(T)=\operatorname{cl}_M(H). Indeed, T⊆H⊆clM(T)T\subseteq H\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_M(T), which proves both closure inclusions. Hence T∪{e}∈ℬ(M)⇔e∉clM(H).T\cup\{e\}\in\mathcal B(M) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e\notin\operatorname{cl}_M(H). Thus independent triples spanning the same hyperplane have the same bit. Since every hyperplane has a spanning triple, one bit yHy_H for each rank-three hyperplane HH determines all the triple bits of a genuine extension.

Write ℋ(N)\mathcal H(N) for these hyperplanes. In a table for the deletion of ff, the visible hyperplanes are Cf={clN(T):T∈(P\{f}3),rN(T)=3}.C_f=\{\operatorname{cl}_N(T):T\in\binom{P\setminus\{f\}}3, \ r_N(T)=3\}. The closure of such a triple in N\fN\setminus f is clN(T)\{f}\operatorname{cl}_N(T)\setminus\{f\}. Consequently every row of ℛ(N\f)\mathcal R(N\setminus f) is constant on triples assigned the same coordinate in CfC_f, and is zero on dependent triples. Projecting onto one bit per visible hyperplane therefore loses no row information. Let Af⊆{0,1}CfA_f\subseteq\{0,1\}^{C_f} be this projected table, and let A⊆{0,1}ℋ(N)A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal H(N)} be the analogous projection of ℛ(N)\mathcal R(N).

The problem actually solved is 𝒮̂(N)={y∈{0,1}ℋ(N):y|Cf∈Af for every f∈P}\A.\widehat{\mathcal S}(N)= \{y\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal H(N)}: y|_{C_f}\in A_f\text{ for every }f\in P\} \setminus A. Expanding yHy_H back to every independent triple spanning HH, and inserting zeros on dependent triples, takes each solution to one in 𝒮(N)\mathcal S(N). Conversely, Proposition 3.2 makes every solution of 𝒮(N)\mathcal S(N) a genuine matroid extension, so the closure argument above shows that it has a unique such compression. Thus 𝒮̂(N)\widehat{\mathcal S}(N) and 𝒮(N)\mathcal S(N) have exactly the same solutions in different coordinates.

Every hyperplane coordinate is seen by at least one deletion: choose f∉Hf\notin H and a spanning triple of HH. That triple avoids ff, so H∈CfH\in C_f. The union of the coordinate sets CfC_f is therefore all of ℋ(N)\mathcal H(N).

4.4. Why the finite join is exhaustive

It remains to impose all the tables simultaneously. For relations UU and VV on coordinate sets SS and TT, their natural join is the finite relation U⋈V={u∪v:u∈U,v∈V,u|S∩T=v|S∩T}.U\Join V=\{u\cup v:u\in U,\ v\in V, \ u|_{S\cap T}=v|_{S\cap T}\}. In words, take every pair of rows that agree on their shared coordinates and combine them into a row on S∪TS\cup T. Grouping rows by their overlap values implements exactly this operation.

Start with the single empty assignment and join the ten relations AfA_f, one at a time. After processing any collection of deletions, the current rows are exactly the assignments on the coordinates seen so far that satisfy all the processed relations. This is true initially, and the definition of join proves the induction step. After all ten joins, every coordinate has been seen, so the rows are exactly {y:y|Cf∈Af for every f∈P}.\{y:y|_{C_f}\in A_f\text{ for every }f\in P\}. Removing the rows in AA now gives 𝒮̂(N)\widehat{\mathcal S}(N) and hence, by the recovery theorem, every member of 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N).

The implementation may change the join order and remove rows of AA as soon as all coordinates have appeared. Neither change alters the result: a completed assignment cannot be changed by later joins, only accepted or rejected. Resource limits are handled separately. An interrupted or bounded calculation is recorded as UNKNOWN, and cannot count as an exhaustive parent search. Only a finished calculation establishes that every solution has been considered. A second method solves the same finite relations using SAT; its encoding is described in Appendix A.3.

Step 2 is therefore justified: complete representation lists give complete tables, and completed joins give exactly 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N). At this stage every output is nonquinary and all its deletions are quinary. The sole remaining minor conditions are the contractions.

5. Step 3: testing contractions and certifying the list

Proposition 2.2 tells us how to finish the classification once the sets 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N) have been recovered. We must test every contraction and then pass from labelled matroids to isomorphism classes. We also verify each final object by certificates that can be checked without rerunning the search.

5.1. The remaining minor conditions

For a simple rank-four matroid MM, every contraction M/aM/a has rank three. It may have parallel elements. Its simplification si(M/a)\operatorname{si}(M/a) deletes loops and keeps one element of each parallel class.

Lemma 5.1 (The contraction test). For M∈𝒟(N)M\in\mathcal D(N), the matroid MM is an excluded minor if and only if every si(M/a)\operatorname{si}(M/a) is a quinary rank-three matroid.

Proof. A matroid is quinary if and only if its simplification is. One direction follows because a simplification is a restriction; for the other, extend a representation of the simplification by zero columns for loops and scalar copies of columns for parallel elements. Thus the stated condition is equivalent to all contractions of MM being quinary. Membership in 𝒟(N)\mathcal D(N) already supplies nonrepresentability of MM and representability of all its deletions. The immediate-minor criterion from Section 1 now gives the result. ▫\square

This is where the rank-three represented catalogue from Step 1 is used. Each simplification has at most ten elements. A simple rank-three matroid on three elements is free and is represented by I3I_3. At sizes four through ten, exact isomorphism comparison with the corresponding complete quinary catalogue decides the test. In particular, this step needs no assumption about a classification of rank-three excluded minors of arbitrary size.

After applying this test, Proposition 2.2 shows that the retained matroids are exactly the required excluded minors, with possible repetitions from their different parents or labellings. Exact matroid isomorphism removes these repetitions. The final distinctness check also uses colored incidence graphs: one color consists of element vertices, the other of vertices for all bases or, when fewer, all nonbases, with edges given by membership. A color-preserving graph isomorphism induces exactly a matroid isomorphism, since either subset family determines the bases. Canonical forms of these graphs therefore give an exact second check of pairwise nonisomorphism.

5.2. Certifying that every listed object is an excluded minor

The construction proves coverage of the target class. For the opposite inclusion, each final basis family is validated and given two kinds of evidence: representations of all its immediate minors, and a proof that the matroid itself has no representation.

The positive evidence is direct. For each a∈E(M)a\in E(M) we save matrices for M\aM\setminus a and M/aM/a, in the order of their remaining labels. An independent modular Gaussian-elimination routine computes the basis family of each matrix and compares it with that of the required minor. It checks every subset of the appropriate size, so agreement establishes equality of the represented matroid and the specified minor. Sage performs a second matrix check. These witnesses certify all the proper-minor conditions using only finite-field arithmetic.

Nonrepresentability requires a different form of evidence. We turn existence of a representation into a finite propositional formula ΦM\Phi_M and check a certificate that this formula is unsatisfiable. Here is the mathematical condition encoded by the formula. Choose a basis B=(b1,…,br)B=(b_1,\ldots,b_r) of MM and reduce its columns to the identity. The remaining entries form a block ZZ, and fundamental exchanges prescribe its support: Zij≠0⇔(B\{bi})∪{j}∈ℬ(M),j∉B.Z_{ij}\ne0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (B\setminus\{b_i\})\cup\{j\}\in\mathcal B(M),\qquad j\notin B. The unknown nonzero entries range over {1,2,3,4}\{1,2,3,4\}. For every rr-subset SS, the determinant of the columns on SS must be nonzero exactly when S∈ℬ(M)S\in\mathcal B(M). These conditions impose the entire basis family.

Proposition 5.2 (Meaning of the nonrepresentability certificate). The exact finite-field encoding ΦM\Phi_M of these determinant conditions is satisfiable if and only if MM is quinary.

Proof. Any representation of MM can be reduced on BB to identity form. Its remaining entries have the prescribed support and satisfy every determinant condition, so they give a solution. Conversely, a solution supplies a full-rank matrix whose nonsingular rr-column submatrices are exactly ℬ(M)\mathcal B(M); this matrix represents MM. The encoding uses exact addition and multiplication tables modulo five, so its satisfying assignments are precisely the solutions of these field conditions. The harmless scaling normalization and the arithmetic clauses are justified in Appendix A. ▫\square

For each final matroid, the SAT solver produces a DRAT refutation of ΦM\Phi_M. DRAT-trim checks this refutation against the saved formula [5]. By Proposition 5.2, a successful check proves that MM is nonquinary. Together with the 22 minor matrices, it certifies that MM is an excluded minor. These certificates use the final basis records directly; they do not need the deletion tables or their enumeration results.

At this point the logical roles are distinct. Complete parent generation and exhaustive recovery show that every excluded minor reaches the final list. Certificates show that every entry of that list is an excluded minor. Exact isomorphism tests determine how many different classes the list represents. We now give the computational facts that establish these statements for the present rank and size.

A. Details of the representation and SAT encodings

The main argument uses matrix normalization to enumerate complete labelled representation sets, and an exact field encoding to certify nonrepresentability. A.1. A normal form for labelled representations

Fix an ordered basis B=(b1,…,br)B=(b_1,\ldots,b_r) of a labelled matroid QQ. Reduce a representation on BB so that its basis columns form IrI_r, and call the remaining block ZZ. The support of ZZ is determined by fundamental exchanges: Zij≠0⇔(B\{bi})∪{j}∈ℬ(Q).Z_{ij}\ne0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (B\setminus\{b_i\})\cup\{j\}\in\mathcal B(Q). Its support graph has one vertex for each row, one for each nonbasis column, and an edge for each nonzero entry. Fix a deterministic spanning forest of this graph. By row and column scaling we make every forest entry equal to 1, compensating on basis columns to preserve IrI_r.

Lemma A.1 (Normal form). With the basis and support forest fixed, this normalization gives one matrix for each labelled projective equivalence class.

Proof. To establish existence, root each nontrivial tree at a row vertex and choose its scale. Traverse the tree: whenever the scale at one endpoint of an edge is known, the nonzero entry on that edge uniquely determines the scale at the other endpoint making the entry 1. There is no cycle in the tree, so no conflicting choice is imposed. Isolated vertices require no condition. Inverse scaling of the basis columns restores the identity.

For uniqueness, two projectively equivalent representations reduced on the same labelled basis differ by row and column scalings: the row map must be diagonal because it fixes each basis-column direction. Suppose both have forest entries 1. On every tree edge, the row multiplier times the column multiplier must be 1. Connectivity then forces a common row multiplier throughout a component and its inverse on all column vertices in that component. Their product is also 1 on every nonforest edge, so all entries agree. This proves uniqueness, including when the support graph is disconnected. ▫\square

In the labelled-orbit calculation of Lemma 4.1, every new matrix is normalized before comparison with those already seen. The lemma proves that this comparison removes exactly projectively equivalent repetitions. The full automorphism group of QQ is obtained from the colored element–basis/nonbasis incidence graph described in Section 5. Its action on element vertices is exactly Aut(Q)\operatorname{Aut}(Q), which supplies the generators used in the orbit calculation.
